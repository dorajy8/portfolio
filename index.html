AI & Machine Learning

RAG Chatbot for UChicago MSADS

Generative AI Principles · UChicago MSADS team project

My role: Frontend & demo — Streamlit UI, chat interaction testing and debugging, and the live-demo flow.

The Problem

Applicants normally navigate 14 separate MSADS pages to answer simple questions about admissions, tuition, curriculum, and capstone requirements. We built a grounded RAG chatbot that retrieves the correct passage, cites its source, and declines to answer anything the corpus does not support.

Pipeline

  • Scraped 14 MSADS sub-pages with requests and BeautifulSoup.
  • Chunked into 1,500-character sliding windows with 100-character overlap.
  • Embedded with all-mpnet-base-v2, producing 768-dimensional vectors.
  • Retrieved via ChromaDB cosine search, top-k = 4 (adjustable), returning text, title, and URL.
  • Assembled a context of at most 3,000 characters, then generated with GPT-4o / Llama 3.2 at temperature 0.2 and a 900-token cap.

Guardrails

  • Grounding: every answer must tie to retrieved evidence, and the model may not invent program details.
  • Failure policy: if retrieval fails, the bot answers “I don't know” rather than guessing.
  • Scope filter: off-topic queries get a polite redirect before any API call is made.
  • PII redaction: a regex pass strips every email address and phone number from LLM output before display.

Challenges We Hit

  • The chatbot too often replied “I don't have enough information.” We wrote a custom cleaner, re-chunked by topic, and expanded the corpus from 9 to 14 pages.
  • Two questions in one prompt broke retrieval, because the retriever embedded the whole multi-question prompt as a single vector. Fixed with multi-query retrieval and query decomposition.
  • Retrieval recall alone did not catch hallucinations or bad refusals, so we evaluated behavior using a harness-agent evaluator instead of recall metrics.

AI & Machine Learning

Predicting Clothing Fit

A Machine Learning Approach to Size Recommendation · March 2026

Team: Harleen Kaur Buttar, Dora Jiayue Li, Skylar Liu — Machine Learning 1

The Problem

Size recommendation and fit prediction improve online shopping and cut returns. This project builds a pipeline that identifies body-type segments, predicts fit outcomes, and recommends the optimal size for a given customer–product pair.

Data

  • Rent The Runway — 192,544 rental transactions with fit feedback, body measurements, review text, ratings, and occasion context. Our primary modeling dataset.
  • ModCloth — 82,790 purchase transactions with partial measurements, used to test cross-retailer generalization.
  • Clothes-Size-Prediction — 119,218 records of clean height, weight, and age mapped to XXS–XXXL, used as a benchmarking baseline.

Cleaning standardized height, weight, and bust into numeric features and computed BMI, converted everything to inches and pounds, filtered unrealistic ages and measurements, one-hot encoded the categorical feedback fields, and dropped columns more than 80% null.

Phase 1 — Unsupervised Segmentation

K-means, K-modes, DBSCAN, and GMM clustering to find the dimensions along which fit fails: who the customers are and what they are renting. LDA topic modeling translated unstructured review language into structured probability distributions that became features for the next phase.

Phase 2 — Fit Classification

Fit outcome as a three-class problem (Small / Fit / Large) with imbalanced labels. Seven classifiers were trained and compared on accuracy and macro-F1, run against both a simple feature set (weight, age, height) and the engineered RTR features, so we could measure exactly how much richer features bought us.

Phase 3 — Size Recommender

Collaborative filtering over historical fit data: a sparse user × category-size matrix where each entry holds the probability that a size fits that user, with cosine similarity to find users with similar fit patterns. A category-level size popularity prior stabilizes predictions when interaction data is sparse and handles cold-start users with no history.

Limitations

  • Fit labels are self-reported and reflect subjective perception, not objective measurement.
  • Sizing is inconsistent between manufacturers, and garment dimensions are missing.
  • No temporal dimension, so the model cannot account for fit perception shifting over seasons.
  • The recommender underperformed on RTR because it was trained on normalized categories rather than item IDs.

Amazon Review Similarity Analysis

Big Data and Cloud Computing · solo project

The Question

As AI gets better at writing coherently like a human, it becomes easier to generate a batch of fake reviews to promote a product. This project analyzes two Amazon datasets spanning 2000 to 2023 — 285 months in total — to investigate whether AI contributes to redundant product reviews, and whether that redundancy can still be caught with scalable similarity-based analysis.

Data Preparation

Joined two datasets, removed records missing categories, ratings, product titles, timestamps, or join keys, dropped columns with large numbers of nulls, and kept only the columns essential to analyzing purchases, reviews, ratings, and prices. Deduplication on timestamp and user ID found roughly 1% duplicate records.

What the Timeline Showed

  • Very few reviews between 2000 and 2012, then a steady climb as online shopping took hold.
  • Volume peaked in December 2019 and January 2020 as COVID pushed lifestyles onto delivery, with a second spike in March and April 2021 overlapping lockdown.
  • As price rose, review count fell but average rating rose — expensive products draw fewer buyers but more consistently positive feedback.
  • The most-reviewed products clustered in phone accessories and beauty items, and active reviewers who post often still rate generously, mostly on products under $25.

The Similarity Finding

Across 8,058 titles, 45.45% of reviews had a similar title, while only 7.40% had similar body text — titles are short and repetitive, so they collide far more often than prose does.

The counterintuitive result came from comparing 5,103 recent reviews (2022–2023) against 5,061 older ones (pre-2022): the recent reviews duplicated less. LSH detects lexical duplication, not semantic similarity. AI-generated reviews are lexically unique even when they say the same thing, so the duplication rate drops precisely where you would expect it to rise. That is a limitation of the method, not evidence of authenticity.

Yelp Restaurant Recommendation System

Machine Learning 2 · team project

Team: Charlotte Zhang, Dora Li, Emily Xue, Mia Mac

The Problem

Restaurant discovery is a high-frequency decision: people choose where to eat several times a week, yet most platforms surface options by proximity or average rating, leaving individual taste patterns largely unexploited. That creates a persistent gap between what platforms recommend and what a given user would actually enjoy. Using the Yelp Open Dataset — reviews, ratings, profile features, cuisine type, price range, ambience, and amenities — our objective was to close it with a personalized ranked list.

Approach

We built a ladder of architectures where each step addressed the previous model's bottleneck: item-item and user-user collaborative filtering established the baseline, content features broke through the sparsity ceiling, and LightGCN's graph propagation added multi-hop signal.

Results

  • +38% NDCG@10 going from item-item CF to LightGCN (0.026 → 0.036).
  • Richer representation did improve ranking, confirming our first hypothesis. Graph structure beat features: LightGCN's 3-hop propagation carried signal that content features alone could not.
  • Our second hypothesis — that user activity predicts CF quality — was only partially confirmed. It held for item-item CF and LightGCN, but user-user CF was non-monotonic, with NDCG@10 actually dipping for users with 6–20 reviews compared to those with 1–5.

Future Work

  • A content-based cold-start fallback on categories, location, and price range to cover new users until collaborative signal accumulates.
  • Pre-computed embeddings indexed with FAISS for sub-millisecond retrieval, making LightGCN viable at production scale.
  • Re-evaluation on 2023–2024 data: our test set's 47% cold-start removal rate was driven by pandemic closures, so a later window would give a cleaner baseline.

Forecasting Chicago 311 Service Demand

Time Series Analysis · team project

Team: Elaine Wang, Dora Jiayue Li, Yuwei Tang, Tallulah Cook, Xiaoyan Chen

The Problem

Chicago's 311 system routes hundreds of thousands of service requests monthly across city departments. Without reliable demand forecasts, agencies cannot efficiently allocate staffing or resources, which leads to delayed response times, resident dissatisfaction, and budget inefficiency. Success meant a model recommendation balancing forecast accuracy against interpretability — one the city could realistically adopt for day-to-day planning.

Data

  • Daily 311 request volume, 2020–2026, viewed both citywide and by the top five departments: 311 City Services, Alderman, CDOT, Streets & Sanitation, and DWM.
  • Monthly weather from NOAA NCEI Climate at a Glance — average temperature and temperature anomaly.

Processing parsed and sorted dates onto a continuous index, reindexed to daily counts with count-safe handling of missing days, built monthly aggregates for robustness and to align with weather frequency, then joined the two. EDA showed strong weekly seasonality, which justified a 7-day seasonal period for the daily models.

Models Compared

A seasonal-naive baseline (forecast tomorrow using last week's same weekday), SARIMA for trend and seasonal structure, SARIMAX adding weather as external regressors, Prophet for trend and holiday effects, Holt-Winters exponential smoothing, and VAR for multivariate structure — all scored on MAE and RMSE.

Findings

  • The best model varies by aggregation level and time granularity. For daily operational forecasting, Prophet was best for total 311 volume, while Holt-Winters and Prophet led at the department level.
  • Translated to operations, the forecast pre-stages resources ahead of predicted surges, shifts staffing by department before peak days, and triggers cross-department coordination when several departments spike at once.

Limitations

  • Feature sets were inconsistent across models — some used weather regressors, others did not.
  • A single train/test split with insufficient cross-validation.
  • Mixed daily and monthly granularity limits direct MAE/RMSE comparison between models.

Recurrent Spiking Neural Network Research

Under the lead of Prof. Yuqing Zhu, I worked with four other students during summer 2024 to research RSNN in terms of its potential to complete tasks and its functional similarities with how our brain carries out information processing.

We found that compared to backpropagation, using evolutionary algorithms to train RSNN pushes the recurrent layer of the network to perform brain-like behavior, which represents the desired brain activities that neuroscientists want to observe. Antara and I presented our results at the Society of Neuroscience conference in Chicago 2024.
